# The Order Book Set Records While the Stock Fell 40%. I've Seen This Movie Before.

*OSI Systems de-rated from 27x to 17x while its backlog hit a new high every quarter. The last time it
sat flat and cheap like this, a government super-cycle took it from $85 to $310. The catch is in the
word "recognized."*

Primary Source Arbitrage · November 2026

I found OSI Systems on a small-cap screen and almost scrolled past it. What stopped me was one chart I
built by hand: the backlog, quarter by quarter, making a new high at the same time the stock was cut
nearly in half. That is not supposed to happen. A company's order book is the market's favorite forward
indicator, and here it was setting records into a 40% drawdown. I pulled the numbers three times
because I assumed I had a data error. I didn't. The gap between those two lines is the whole post, and
closing it means understanding one word the market cares about more than "backlog." That word is
**recognized**.

## The short version

- **What happened.** OSIS went from $311 in April to $186 in August and sits near $200. Like-for-like
  that's a 37% de-rate, forward P/E from ~27x to ~17x. Over the same stretch, backlog rose every
  quarter to a record $2.1B and book-to-bill hit 1.4 in the September quarter.
- **Why.** A momentum stock priced at 34x trailing delivered a merely fine year. A big government
  revenue program rolled off faster than the next one ramped, service growth slowed, a Middle East war
  slipped deliveries, and a US shutdown delayed orders. The business didn't break. The growth paused,
  and a growth multiple can't survive a pause.
- **The pattern.** This has happened before, in reverse. The last government super-cycle took OSI from
  ~$85 to ~$310 as it was won and recognized, then handed back 38% as it rolled off. A bigger, funded
  one is appropriated and waiting. I'll show you the chart.
- **What it's priced for.** Run the DCF backwards and the market is paying for about 6% growth, the low
  end of its own 6-9% consensus. Not cheap, not a steal. The margin of safety here is the business, not
  the multiple.
- **What I hold.** A 9-share starter, entered near $199 in November. I plan to add toward the 200-week
  line near $177 if it's offered, and scale on the January and April prints if they confirm.
- **What proves me wrong.** The April 2027 print. Revenue under a +10% bar, flat margins, and a third
  Middle East slip would tell me the pause is a stall.

This is a starter near a floor with a dated test, not a table-pound. The honest expected value is only
modestly positive. I'll show you the math and the bear case, because on this one the bear is good.

## The two lines

Coverage is the cleaner signal than the raw backlog number, because it adjusts for the size of the
business. Backlog divided by trailing revenue sat at roughly 1.05x for a year and a half, then jumped to
**1.16x in the September quarter**, a multi-year high. Rising coverage means the order book is growing
faster than revenue, so forward visibility is improving, not just holding. For a lumpy
government-contract business, 1.16x is about fourteen months of revenue already contracted.

There's a harder number underneath it. In the Q1 FY27 10-Q, OSI disclosed remaining performance
obligations of about **$2.0B, with roughly 64% expected to convert inside twelve months** — about
$1.28B of next year's revenue already under contract. RPO can still slip on timing, but it carries its
own clock, which backlog doesn't.

So the forward demand is real and improving. And the stock fell anyway. That isn't a contradiction. It's
the most important mechanic in the business.

## Backlog floors the business. Only recognition moves the price.

A booked order improves the backlog today. It improves *earnings* only when the product ships and the
revenue is recognized, which for this company is a year or two later, on a government timeline, sometimes
through an active war zone. The market stopped paying for the order book the moment it doubted the
conversion. Rising backlog floored the business. It could not lift the price, because price follows the
income statement, and the income statement was being told to wait.

If you own the stock, that distinction is the whole game. A booked, funded order is value accruing to
the business whether or not the market claps. I tested it: over the last five years, OSI's year-end
backlog converted to next-year revenue at almost exactly 1.0x, every year, and backlog, revenue and EPS
compounded together at 12%, 9% and 14%. The order book is not a vanity number here. It becomes revenue
on a lag. The re-rate is the bonus, and it arrives when recognition re-proves the story. If you're
trading the stock, backlog is noise until a print turns it into earnings. If you're an owner, the gap
between a funded order and a recognized dollar is exactly where you get paid to wait.

Which brings me to the part of this I find genuinely compelling, because it has already happened once.

## I've seen this movie: the last super-cycle

Forget service revenue and margin mix for a minute. The thing that actually moved this stock, up and
down, was a government contract super-cycle recognizing and then rolling off. Here is the tape.

[Exhibit: OSI price vs the Mexico super-cycle. 2020-2022 range-bound $75-102. 2022 Mexico contracts won
(~$800M), stock ~$85. 2023-2025 recognition ramp, $118 → $138 → $225. April 2026 peak ~$310 at ~34x
trailing. May 2026 crash to $240 as Mexico revenue fell from $69M to $11M. August low $186. Source:
LSEG daily prices; Mexico contract figures from company filings and the BofA fireside.]

OSI won three Mexican security contracts worth north of $800M around 2022. The stock was sitting at
about $85, where it had drifted for two flat years. Then the revenue began to recognize — fiscal 2024,
2025, into 2026 — and the stock ran to **$310, a 3.6x**, as earnings compounded and the multiple
expanded to 34x. At the top, everyone had a thesis: the recurring-service story, the record backlog, the
defense-radar acquisition. Then Mexico rolled off. Quarterly Mexico revenue collapsed from $69M to $11M,
service growth slowed at the same moment, the war slipped deliveries, and the stock gave back 38%. The
narrative strands people were pointing at were real, but they were symptoms. The engine was the
super-cycle, and the super-cycle recognized, peaked, and faded.

That chart is the thesis and the warning at once.

The thesis: **there is a bigger super-cycle already appropriated.** The One Big Beautiful Bill put over
$1B into Customs and Border Protection non-intrusive inspection, and the Secure America Act locked
roughly $26B of CBP funding through fiscal 2029 via reconciliation — hard to claw back even after a
change in Congress. OSI has already won the contract vehicles: two CBP IDIQs with ceilings of $200M and
$85M, and it has historically taken more than 40% of this kind of award. This money recognizes in FY28
and beyond. **Right now you are buying in the gap** — Mexico off the books, CBP not yet on them — at the
median multiple, with the stock flat and cheap, which is exactly where it sat in 2022 before the last
one recognized.

The warning is on the same chart: the people who bought at $310 and 34x, at full recognition, lost 40%.
Super-cycles are cyclical. You want to be early, in the gap, not late, at the peak. I am buying the
2022-analog setup and writing myself a rule to trim when CBP is fully in the numbers and the multiple is
rich again, somewhere around FY29-30. Don't chase the recognition once it's in the price.

## Is it cheap? No — and I'm not going to pretend otherwise

I wanted this to be a cheap stock. It isn't, and the easy, wrong version of this post would say it is. So
I built the thing the sell-side doesn't publish: OSI's P/E band back to 2010, from 29 years of daily
price and 20 years of fiscal EPS.

[Exhibit: forward and trailing P/E band by year, 2010-2026. Forward median ~18x, trailing ~21x. Troughs
11-14x in 2020 and 2022. Peaks 25-32x+ in 2013, 2016, 2017, 2019, 2025. Today ~17x forward, ~21x
trailing. Source: LSEG price history and non-GAAP EPS.]

The band settles two arguments at once. The bull claim that 17x is cheap is weak: on forward earnings
the median is about 18x, so you are at the median, not a discount. The "cheap" feeling is only against
the 2024-25 froth. But the bear claim that OSI "deserves" 13-15x, that the market has finally figured it
out, is weaker still. That level is the 2020 and 2022 trough, printed in a pandemic and a rate shock. It
is the floor, not fair value. The ten-year median is ~21x, and this stock has traded 25-32x repeatedly,
across cycles, whenever the growth was visible. So the multiple is fair both ways. A re-rate from 17x to
20x is reversion to the median, not a stretch, and the market has a decade-long habit of granting it
when earnings re-accelerate. **The margin of safety is the business — the funded backlog and the floor —
not the price.**

One more anchor, with a correction I owe you. My first comp was embarrassing: I called OSI cheap against
Smiths at 22x. But Smiths sold its detection arm to CVC, a private-equity buyer, on June 30, so the 22x
public company is now industrial seals and heating elements, unrelated to detection. The right comp is
the transaction: CVC paid about **12.5x EBITDA** (£2B enterprise value on ~£160M EBITDA) for the pure-
play. OSI, the global #1 in cargo inspection, trades at ~12.2x. A sophisticated buyer just set a
real-money price on the exact asset class, and the public leader trades in line with it. OSI is not a
pure-play — it's roughly 70% detection, with optoelectronics and a small healthcare arm — but with
Smiths Detection now private, Leidos a services business, and China's Nuctech banned from the core
Western market, **OSI is close to the only sizable public way to own detection at scale.** That gives it
a takeout floor, too.

## What the market is actually pricing

Run the DCF backwards — properly, two-stage, not the single-stage shortcut that flatters you. At ~$200,
on a 9% cost of capital I actually built up (11% cost of equity, cheap fixed-rate convertible debt), the
price implies roughly **6% near-term growth fading to 3% terminal.** Consensus has revenue up 6-9%
through FY29, coverage rising, and the FY28-29 funding appropriated. So the market is pricing the **low
end of its own consensus.** The edge isn't a secret contract nobody saw; the CBP awards and the backlog
are public and partly in the stock. The edge is narrower and more honest: the market is anchored on the
near-term reset and the war and is under-weighting a contracted, improving order book. You're paid for
6% and buying a business set up to do 7-9% if the backlog converts. Modest mispricing, contingent on
conversion. Not a scream.

## The bear case, at full strength

If I were short this, the case is good, and I won't wave it away.

OSI is a lumpy government hardware contractor whose gross margin has quietly slid from ~36% in 2017 to
~33%, with the Street modeling it flat. The entire return requires a re-rate whose *timing* is undated —
you can sit at 17x for a year earning nothing but a shrinking share count. The 2H ramp everyone
underwrites has already slipped twice: down 1.8% in one quarter, down 12% in the next. The easy comps
that make this year's ramp "achievable" exist precisely because management missed. Q1 looked great on
EPS, up 17%, but GAAP operating income actually fell year-over-year, from about $33.6M to $33.1M, and
gross margin was down. The EPS gain was lower interest and a smaller share count — and the buyback was
partly funded by a one-time Mexico cash harvest that ends, which is why consensus free cash flow steps
down from ~$209M in FY27 to ~$162M in FY28. And there's no informational edge: the stock is ~90%
institutionally owned, with BlackRock, Fidelity and Vanguard already in it. If it were obviously cheap,
they'd have bid it to 20x. They haven't. The bear's verdict: a fairly valued, margin-declining,
timing-dependent box-seller whose upside is a re-rate the market has no obligation to grant and whose
catalyst has failed twice. There is no margin of safety, just a story about next year — again.

That's the honest downside, and it's why this is a starter and not a full position. My rebuttal is
narrow. The backlog and coverage are objective and rising. Q1 was a clean revenue beat, $403.5M against
$393M expected. The historical band destroys the "deserves 13-15x" line. The funding is appropriated
through 2029. And the super-cycle chart shows the market *does* re-rate this name when recognition
arrives — it just won't pre-pay for it after being burned. But I can't date the re-rate, so I size it so
being wrong until April doesn't hurt.

While I'm confessing, a few more of my own first-draft overstatements, because the thesis got better
when I stopped overselling it. I wrote "margin-expansion flywheel"; margins are modeled flat, so the
trade is revenue plus operating leverage plus buybacks, not expanding margins. I wrote "legally locked
funding"; the honest word is visibility — the money is appropriated but spread across vendors and uses,
and OSI's awards are IDIQ *ceilings*, not spend. And I leaned too hard on a service-revenue recovery:
service is only a quarter of revenue, almost all of it inside Security, and its margin lever is small.
Management has guided it back to double digits, and the Street hasn't modeled the benefit — so it's free
upside if it shows — but it is not the thesis. The thesis is the super-cycle.

## What I hold, and what would change my mind

A 9-share starter near $199, entered in November. I'll add toward the 200-week near $177 if the market
offers it — the stock is a thin name with a wide spread, so I use limits — and scale on the January and
April prints if they confirm. There is no dividend; the ~5.6% free-cash-flow yield isn't paid to me, it
funds buybacks that shrink the count, so "paid to wait" means the business compounds per-share value
while I hold, not that I collect a coupon. The base case is roughly $218 on FY28 consensus at a held
17x, about +11% over a year and a half with no re-rate at all. The bull case needs the re-rate and gets
to the high $200s. The severe bear, if the ramp fails and operating leverage reverses, is around $134.
Probability-weighted, the expected value is only modestly positive. I'm fine with that, because the
downside is mostly "wait," not "lose."

## The close

I'm not paying up for an inflection I can't see yet. I'm buying a floored business at a fair multiple, in
the quiet gap between two government super-cycles, and letting the backlog do the waiting. The order book
has been right for a year. The last time this stock sat flat and cheap before the revenue recognized, it
tripled. It also crashed at the top, so the discipline is to be early and leave before the credits.
