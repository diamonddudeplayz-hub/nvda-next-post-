# Everyone Is Watching the Wrong Number

*Michael Burry rolled his NVIDIA short toward the end of the memory shortage. The shortage will end, rent will fall, and some neoclouds will not survive it. None of that is a short on NVIDIA. The short on NVIDIA is a bet that the AI labs stop paying, and this week the market showed you where that bet trades. Not in the rent index. In the credit market.*

---

For two weeks the market has been running Burry's experiment for him, and the results are not the ones his trade needs.

Since October 8 the AI complex has sold off hard. By October 16, CoreWeave was down about 30% and Nebius about 29% from their October 7 highs, CoreWeave's cost of default insurance had hit a record, and some of its bonds yielded 16%. NVIDIA fell about 9%. That ordering held every day of the selloff, and it is the whole argument. The companies most exposed to who pays for the compute took the damage first. The company that sells the chips took a third of it.

[C2: NVDA, CoreWeave and Nebius indexed to October 7, the lessors falling three times as far as the chipmaker.]

The trigger was not GPU rent, which is what Burry is short. On October 8, OpenAI told investors it had reached about $50 billion of annualized revenue, against the $68 to $70 billion widely reported. The gap was an accounting line, gross versus net, not a demand miss, and OpenAI was still growing 77% a quarter. The market sold anyway, and then it kept selling, down the chain of everyone who had borrowed against the labs' promises to pay. The scare was never about what a used chip is worth. It was about money.

That is the whole post in one observation. NVIDIA is the largest weight in the S&P 500, so if you own an index fund you own this question, and almost everyone is answering the wrong version of it. The question is not what happens to rent when the shortage ends. It is whether the people renting the compute can keep paying. Those are different numbers, they move in different places, and only one of them reaches NVIDIA.

## The short version

- **What happened:** Since October 8 the AI trade sold off in order of who holds the financing risk. CoreWeave and Nebius fell about 30% and CoreWeave's default insurance hit a record, while NVIDIA fell about 9%. The trigger was lab and lessor financing, not a fall in rent.
- **Why the shortage ending barely touches NVIDIA:** On the deals the neoclouds are signing now, a gigawatt of compute brings in $20 to $25 million per megawatt a year and pays back its chips in about two years, against a six-year life. Rent would have to fall by half before a chip stops covering its cost, and rent is rising. The building keeps its power, the cloud swaps in NVIDIA's next chip, and NVIDIA sells every upgrade at a rising price.
- **The number that matters:** Whether the labs keep paying. This week it split in two. Anthropic told investors it is now profitable and makes $1.70 of revenue for every $1 of compute. CoreWeave's credit market priced a better-than-even chance it defaults.
- **What I hold:** NVIDIA.
- **What would prove me wrong:** A neocloud that cannot refinance, or OpenAI's funding round shrinking, or ACIE revenue falling quarter over quarter. Not a markdown in what the old chips are worth.

## Why the shortage ending can't touch NVIDIA

Burry's bet needs rent to fall. It will. The last time a memory shortage broke, the H100 index fell 44% in five months. The mistake is assuming that reaches the company that sells the chips, and it does not, because the scarce asset is not the chip. It is the building with power running to it.

You can see which asset is scarce in who is securing it. A gas turbine ordered today is quoted for 2031, transformers run three to five years, and power interconnection takes years more. The chip inside is the fast part, swapped every few years. Microsoft just told you how it values the two: on its July call it extended the depreciation of its data centers from 15 years to 25, while it writes the servers inside them off over as little as two to six. NVIDIA's own 10-Q guarantees the "land, power, and shell" of its cloud partners' leases, not their chips. Both companies are putting their balance sheets behind the building. The silicon is the consumable.

So a neocloud is a bet on a powered site, and the chip is the part it refreshes. That turns Burry's 1968 analogy inside out. The leasing companies died when IBM's next machine made the old one unrentable, but here the old chip does not have to hold its rent. It has to be replaced, and every replacement is a sale for NVIDIA, at a rising price: its revenue per gigawatt has gone from $18 billion with Hopper to $25 billion with Blackwell to $40 billion with Rubin, and the roadmap carries it to $60 billion with Feynman.

The only question is whether the refresh pays, and the neoclouds' own books answer it. Nebius said in August its multi-year deals yield $20 to $25 million per megawatt with a payback under two years, and IREN said the same, with three-year pricing up 125% since November. A gigawatt earning $20 to $25 billion a year pays back $40 billion of Rubin chips in about two years, against a six-year life. **For the upgrade to stop making sense, rent has to fall by half, and right now it is rising.** A 44% fall, the worst the H100 has ever shown, lands inside that cushion, not through it. The shortage ending takes out a scarcity premium the business does not need.

## The number that matters

Everything above says the same thing: NVIDIA keeps selling as long as the clouds keep buying, and the clouds keep buying as long as their customers keep paying. The customers are the AI labs, and that is the one link that can actually break. NVIDIA discloses in its 10-Q that five direct customers are 70% of its receivables, up from three at 56% a year earlier, and those customers' own revenue runs back to two labs, OpenAI and Anthropic. Kress said on the August call that the labs NVIDIA expects to back with its own balance sheet are roughly a quarter of next year's business. A quarter of NVIDIA leans on two companies.

This week those two told opposite halves of the story. Anthropic, days before its November IPO, said its revenue reached $16.8 billion in the quarter, that it was profitable on an adjusted basis, and that it makes $1.70 of revenue for every $1 it spends on compute. A lab that lost more than $8 billion last year is, on its own account, now paying its way. OpenAI is the other half: its own projection plans $856 billion of compute spending through 2030 against $278 billion of cumulative negative cash flow, so it depends on raising money, and it is raising it into a market that this week got more expensive. The line I would have written a month ago, that the whole build rests on two labs that both lose money, is no longer true. The labs are diverging, and the risk is concentrating on the one that still burns.

The place that risk shows up is credit, which is exactly what cracked this week. CoreWeave's five-year default insurance hit a record, implying better-than-even odds of default, and its bonds traded to yield 16%. That looks like the thesis breaking. Read the actual debt and it is narrower than it looks. The 16% is on CoreWeave's unsecured parent notes, about $1.25 billion of a roughly $35 billion debt stack, sitting behind everything else, which is why they trade at 80 cents. The money that actually funds the chips is secured against named customer contracts and rated investment grade: the $8.5 billion facility behind its Meta deal is priced at 5.9%, anchored by Blackstone. The credit market is charging hard for the riskiest sliver and leaving the contract-backed core alone, which is the opposite of 2006, when the risk hid behind safe ratings. This is stress priced in daylight, not a default. CoreWeave's stock closed up almost 6% the day its insurance hit a record, after Jensen Huang, whose company owns 10% of it, said its capital costs are falling and it needs no bailout.

Stress is not failure, but it is the thing to watch, and it is the reason I keep saying the number is credit, not rent. A neocloud that cannot roll its debt stops buying chips long before any rent index moves. So the warning does not come from the Silicon Data tape. It comes from CoreWeave's bonds, from the size and price of OpenAI's next raise, and from ACIE revenue, the slice of NVIDIA's business that runs through exactly these buyers, which prints in November.

## Why I'm still long

NVIDIA trades around 14 times forward EV/EBITDA, down from 32 a year ago, and this week's selloff pushed it lower. That is the multiple of a business the market expects to slow, not one it expects to break. If the market genuinely thought a lab failure was coming, the kind that takes a quarter of next year's business, NVIDIA would trade where Micron does, at a single-digit multiple, and it does not, even now. So the one risk that actually reaches NVIDIA is still not in the price. That cuts both ways. If it happens, the stock falls hard and Burry wins, on the multiple rather than on rent. If it does not, the stock is cheap for what it earns.

I am on the second side, because the week that scared everyone improved the fundamentals it was supposed to threaten. Anthropic turned profitable, OpenAI neared $70 billion, rent kept rising, and the only thing that actually broke was the price of insuring the most levered lessor's riskiest bonds. Even Gartner's warning that 80% of neoclouds will not survive to 2030 is a story about consolidation, not lost demand: when a neocloud fails, its powered buildings and its contracts get absorbed by a stronger one, and the compute inside still gets bought from NVIDIA.

The honest risk is that this week's stress becomes next quarter's failure, and I cannot rule it out, so I am not adding with both hands and I am watching the credit tape, not the rent. But the bet is clear. The shortage ending is not a short on NVIDIA. A lab that stops paying is, and that trade lives in CoreWeave's bonds and OpenAI's next round, not in the price of a used H100.

## The close

Burry rolled his puts toward the shortage ending, and he may well get his crash in the shortage. He is just not going to get it in NVIDIA, because the shortage was never NVIDIA's risk. The risk is a lab that runs out of money, and this week the market started pricing it in the one place it shows up first: not the rent on the oldest chip, but the cost of insuring the newest lender's debt. So watch that number. I am. Which do you think moves first, CoreWeave's credit or an H100's rent?
