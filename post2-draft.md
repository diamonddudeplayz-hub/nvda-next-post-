# What Is NVIDIA Worth When the Shortage Ends?

*The shortage ending is not NVIDIA's risk. On the contracts the neoclouds are signing now, a gigawatt of compute pays back its chips in about two years and runs for six, so rent can fall a long way before NVIDIA loses the upgrade sale. The risk is whether the AI labs can keep paying for the tokens, and on October 8 the market showed the order it breaks in.*

---

On October 8, the AI complex sold off in a specific order. CoreWeave fell about 8%. Oracle fell 6%. Micron 4.8%. NVIDIA fell 2.9%, the least of the group, and closed at $230.48.

That order is the whole argument of my last post, playing out on a single tape. The companies most exposed to who pays for the compute took the damage first. The company that sells the chips took it last.

The trigger was not a fall in GPU rent, which is what Michael Burry is short. It was a number about an AI lab. On October 8, OpenAI told investors it had reached about $50 billion in annualized revenue at the end of September. The figure widely reported over the prior week was $68 to $70 billion.

The gap is not a demand miss. It is the difference between gross and net. The $70 billion counts the full value of OpenAI's models wherever they are sold, including through clouds like Azure that book the sale as their own. The $50 billion counts only what OpenAI keeps. Underneath, OpenAI's run rate was still growing about 77% a quarter.

The market sold anyway. It sold a company growing 77% because a number it had been quoting turned out to be measured a different way. What moved the tape was the reminder of how much of this build rests on what the labs can pay.

This is not a niche question. NVIDIA is 7.78% of the S&P 500, the single largest weight in the index. If you own an index fund, you own this question.

Burry has given himself room to be early. In late September, according to Stocktwits, he rolled his NVIDIA short into September 2027 puts struck in the mid-$100s. A later expiry moves his clock toward the 2028 and 2029 supply wave, which is where my last post's memory argument runs out. So this post answers the rolled-forward version of his bet. The question is not whether the shortage ends. It is what NVIDIA is worth when it does.

The conventional answer is clean. Chips stop being scarce, old-chip rent falls, NVIDIA's pricing power erodes, and a bull case built on today's prices comes apart. **My answer is that the shortage ending barely touches NVIDIA, and the thing that actually threatens it is a different event with a different clock: the AI labs running out of money to pay for compute.** Those are two separate bets. Only the second one reaches the stock, and I do not think it lands before Burry's puts expire. The rest of this post is why, with the arithmetic that decides it.

Steve Eisman, who is long NVIDIA himself, put the reason the depreciation fight misses the point on October 2: Burry's useful-life argument is "too academic." I think that is right, and not a dismissal. What a chip is worth is its rent, rent is paid by whoever is renting the compute, and the question that decides NVIDIA is whether those renters keep paying once chips stop being scarce. Eisman and I end up in the same place. The difference is that he says it on television without numbers, and the numbers are the entire argument.

## The short version

- **What happened:** On October 8 a lab revenue reset, not a rent decline, took down the AI complex in order of who holds the financing risk. CoreWeave and Oracle first, NVIDIA last.
- **When the shortage ends:** Memory makers and NVIDIA both say memory and power stay short through 2028. When supply does ease, old-chip rent can fall, as H100 rent fell 44% the last time a shortage broke.
- **Why that barely touches NVIDIA:** The scarce asset is the powered building, not the chip. On the multi-year deals Nebius and IREN are signing now, a gigawatt of compute brings in roughly $20 to $25 million per megawatt a year and pays back its chips in about two years, against a six-year life. Rent would have to fall by half before a chip stops covering its cost, and right now rent is rising. The cloud swaps new chips into the building it already powered, and NVIDIA sells every upgrade at a rising price per gigawatt ($18 billion for Hopper, $25 billion for Blackwell, $40 billion for Rubin, $60 billion for Feynman).
- **Where the real risk is:** Not the old chips' value. It is whether the AI labs, which Kress says NVIDIA expects to back with its own balance sheet at roughly a quarter of next year's business, can keep funding the demand the whole build rests on.
- **What I hold:** NVIDIA.
- **What would prove me wrong:** The labs' money drying up while capacity keeps arriving. It shows up as ACIE revenue falling quarter over quarter (rule 8) and the labs' next funding rounds shrinking or pricing wider, not as a markdown in what the old chips are worth.

## When the shortage ends

Burry's catalyst is the bottleneck easing. My last post made the case that it does not ease before his puts expire, and I will not repeat all of it. The short version is that the people who sell the shortage and the people who buy into it agree on the timing. Micron said on September 30 it has "no line of sight" to when supply and demand return to balance. Samsung said on July 30 it is "unlikely to see any significant increase in incremental supply through 2028." NVIDIA's own CFO, Colette Kress, said on August 26 that supply stays a bottleneck "at least through the end of fiscal year '28," which ends in January 2028.

One point worth being precise about, because it runs through the whole argument: the thing in short supply is memory and power, not NVIDIA's chips. NVIDIA can make plenty of GPUs. What it cannot conjure is high-bandwidth memory and a building with power running to it. That is what holds rent up, and it is why the shortage lasts.

So assume Burry is right about the eventual direction, just early. When a shortage does break, old-chip rent falls, and it can fall hard. Silicon Data's H100 index went from $3.40 an hour in September 2024 to $1.90 by February 2025, down 44% in five months, as the first Hopper shortage ended and new supply arrived. That is the event the bear case needs. The question is what a 44% fall does to NVIDIA, and the answer turns on an asset that is not the chip.

## The scarce asset moved

The thing that is actually hard to get is not the GPU. It is a building with power running to it.

You can see this in the lead times. A gas turbine ordered today is quoted for delivery around 2031, and large transformers run three to five years (a16z, citing TrendForce, September). Power interconnection and permitting take years more. Against that, the chip inside the building is the fast part, swapped every few years. IREN's co-CEO, Daniel Roberts, put the whole thesis in one line on the company's August 27 call: "The digital world scales almost instantly, the physical world does not. Power, land, data centers, these things take years to permit, finance and build."

NVIDIA says the same in its own filings, in the plainest possible terms. Its latest 10-Q books a category called "Land, Power, and Shell Guarantees for AI Clouds," where it backs partners' data-center lease obligations if they default. A guarantee here means NVIDIA agrees to pay the building's lease if the cloud renting it cannot. NVIDIA is willing to put its balance sheet behind the land, the power and the shell. It is not doing that for the chips. That tells you which part it thinks is scarce, and it is also a real exposure, which is why it sits in the ledger at the end.

The buyers are now saying the same thing with their accounting. On its fourth-quarter call on July 29, Microsoft's CFO, Amy Hood, said:

> "Effective at the start of FY '27, we are extending the estimated useful life of our data centers and office buildings from 15 to 25 years, reflecting our operating history and expected use of these assets."

Microsoft's own 10-K depreciates the servers and network equipment inside those buildings over two to six years. So the same company now writes the building off over 25 years and the chips inside it over as little as two, and it stretched the building's life in the same year memory prices were setting records. Management is putting its depreciation schedule where the economics are. The shell is the long-lived asset. The silicon is the consumable.

This is the hinge of Burry's own analogy, turned around. In 1968 the computer-leasing companies were killed when IBM's next machine made the old one hard to re-rent. The equivalent risk today is that NVIDIA's next chip makes the last one worthless. But a neocloud is not only a bet on a chip. It is a bet on a building. Jensen Huang said as much at Goldman Sachs on September 10: the neoclouds "secure land power and shell for us that the CSPs have already exhausted," meaning the cloud service providers have used up the power and land they can easily reach, so the neoclouds go find more. And he was blunt about the fate of old chips once the building outlives them: "You can still rent Voltas. Voltas is 10 years old. All the Ampere in the world are all rented out."

Kress put the economics on the August 26 call, describing a 20-year site, where LPS is that same land, power and shell:

> "The LPS commitment secures a long-lived AI factory site, while the NVIDIA compute within the data center can be upgraded repeatedly."

**That sentence is the difference between Burry's story and this one.** The lessor's durable asset is the powered site. The chip is the part it refreshes, and every refresh is a sale for NVIDIA. And NVIDIA prices each refresh higher: its revenue opportunity per gigawatt has gone from about $18 billion with Hopper to $25 billion with Blackwell to $40 billion with Rubin, and the roadmap carries it to $60 billion with Feynman (Non-Deal Roadshow deck, September 2026, p.11). The building persists. The bill for filling it goes up every generation.

## Does the upgrade pay?

Here is the question that actually answers the post, and until last week I could not close it, because the number you need is what a gigawatt of tokens earns, and the only public figure was Huang's claim that a 1 GW site "is being rented out for" about $50 billion a year. That is the seller's number, and it is the retail, fully-utilized, newest-chip top end.

The neoclouds' own books give the real figure. On its August 12 call, Nebius said its core one-to-three-year deals carry "a yield of $20 million to $25 million per megawatt," with customers prepaying 50% to 60% of the cost and a payback "less than 2 years." IREN, on August 27, said its recent three-year contracts price "in excess of $20 million per megawatt of IT load, paying back the compute investment in around 2 years," with active talks near $25 million. Two independent operators, the same number: **about $20 to $25 million per megawatt of IT load per year, which is $20 to $25 billion per gigawatt, paying back the chips in roughly two years.** Huang's $50 billion is the short-term premium end; Nebius confirms it, quoting $40 to $50 million per megawatt for six-month capacity. The durable, multi-year number is half that, and it is audited.

Now the math closes. NVIDIA's hardware for a gigawatt of Rubin is about $40 billion, and the full build, where GPUs are roughly two-thirds of the cost, is about $60 billion. A gigawatt earning $20 to $25 billion a year pays the chips back in about two years and the whole build in under three, against a chip life of six. After it has paid for itself, it earns for three or four more years. For the upgrade to stop making sense, rent has to fall far enough that a chip no longer covers its cost over those six years, and my last post put that break-even at roughly a 50% to 65% fall in the B200 index.

**Rent is going the other way.** IREN said its three-year contract pricing is up about 125% since November, and Nebius said its first capacity auction cleared 15% above the highest price it had ever charged for Blackwell. A 44% fall, the worst the H100 has ever shown, lands in the middle of that cushion, not through it. And because memory and power stay short through 2028, the scarcity premium comes out slowly, not at once.

So the answer to what happens to NVIDIA after the shortage is that the chip economics have years of room. The thing that closes that room is not oversupply, which the shortage prevents. It is the earns side collapsing, rent falling by half because the demand for tokens fell, and that happens only if the people renting the compute stop paying. Which brings the whole post to one question.

## But only if the money keeps coming

The upgrade cycle, the thing that keeps NVIDIA selling after the shortage, runs on one input: a cloud that can pay for the next chip, out of its own cash or a new loan. Both depend on its customers paying. So the durable building does not break the chain. It moves the question one link down, to who pays.

The machinery for this is real and mostly works. Nebius says customers prepay 50% to 60% of the capital cost and will put up more than $9 billion of prepayments this year, and it has about $40 billion of contracted backlog to borrow against. IREN has turned this into a flywheel: prepayments cover about half the GPU cost, GPU-backed loans cover up to 90% of the rest, and it keeps its data centers unencumbered to finance later. These are companies funding the next building before it is built, which is the opposite of a business that needs old chips to hold their rent.

The weak version is the part Burry is describing, and it exists. CoreWeave's DDTL 5.5, a $2.6 billion loan it closed on August 10, runs about five years against customer contracts that average about three. For the last two years, the lenders get paid only if CoreWeave re-rents those GPUs at a decent price. It priced at SOFR plus 5.50%, about 10.4% all in, a full point wider than the same loan three months earlier at the same ratings, and CoreWeave's five-year credit default swap sat at 8.2% on October 2, about two and a half times a year earlier. The credit market is not hiding this risk the way it hid subprime in 2006. It is charging for it in the open, and the charge is landing on CoreWeave's overall company credit, not inside the individual loans backed by a named customer's contract, which lenders still treat as safe.

It is worth being clear about what blew up in July, because it was not this. A hedge fund running as much as 400% leverage on AI infrastructure stocks got margin-called, and its biggest holdings, Nebius, Micron, Sandisk and CoreWeave, each fell more than 35% as it was forced to sell into Citadel. The leverage that failed sat in stock positions, not in the GPU-backed loans. Twelve days later CoreWeave closed DDTL 5.5 anyway.

So the upgrade is financeable today, at a rising price. Whether it stays financeable is a question about the renters, and the renters are the labs.

## Who pays for the tokens

Start with who buys NVIDIA's chips, because it is more concentrated than the headline suggests. In its latest 10-Q, NVIDIA discloses that five direct customers made up 22%, 14%, 13%, 11% and 10% of its receivables, which is 70% from five accounts. At the fiscal 2026 year-end in January, three customers were 56%. The buyers are concentrating, not diversifying. NVIDIA does not name them, and given its customer categories they are the large cloud providers, system integrators and manufacturers that build for the hyperscalers and neoclouds, not the labs directly.

Those buyers' own revenue is concentrated one layer further, in the labs. I could not source Eisman's claim that about 70% of the hyperscalers' AI cloud revenue comes from OpenAI and Anthropic, so I flag it as his, but what I can source points the same way: CoreWeave's S-1 shows 62% of its 2024 revenue came from Microsoft, and Oracle's cloud backlog is reportedly about half OpenAI. NVIDIA sells to a handful of clouds, and those clouds sell to a handful of labs. The demand narrows as you follow the money down.

Kress named the exposure on the August 26 call. The AI labs "for which we expect to leverage our balance sheet" are, she said, roughly a quarter of NVIDIA's business next year. That is the layer NVIDIA backs directly, through equity stakes, through the $105 billion Ohio lease it guarantees for OpenAI, and through the land, power and shell guarantees. A quarter of the business leans on companies that are not yet profitable.

Whether the labs can carry that depends on something both the bulls and the bears misread: tokens. The bull cites volume, which is enormous and still rising. The bear cites price, which has more than halved, from about $2.06 on Silicon Data's token index in late May to $0.97 on October 1, while rent held roughly flat. Neither one multiplies the two to get the number that matters, which is spend.

[C1: token-price index against H100/B200 rent, the halving while rent held.]

And the multiplication is genuinely hard, because both inputs are moving in ways that mislead. From GPT-6 Astra to the GPT-6.1 Sol release, OpenAI cut the price of comparable capability about fivefold, listing Sol at $2 per million input tokens against Astra's $10. On that one example, usage has to grow fivefold just to hold revenue. But the newer models are reasoning models that burn far more tokens to do the same job, so a chunk of the volume growth is mechanical, the same task costing more tokens, not new paying demand. And a large share of the tokens, over 85% of agent tokens by one count, are cached and priced about 95% below standard. The count overstates demand from both ends.

So you cannot read the labs' health off the price or the count. You have to look at what they take in against what they have promised to spend, and here the numbers are stark. OpenAI's own investor projection, reported by the Financial Times on September 21, plans about $856 billion of compute spending between 2026 and 2030 against revenue that reaches it late, for a cumulative $278 billion of negative free cash flow over the period. It keeps about $50 billion a year today and is raising again, reportedly about $30 billion at a $1.4 trillion valuation. Anthropic, on the prospectus Reuters has seen, did $4.6 billion of revenue in 2025 against an operating loss above $8 billion. The spend is contracted years out. The revenue that has to service it belongs to two companies, neither of which makes a profit.

**This is where Eisman and I agree, and where I part from him.** He is right that the whole build rests on two labs that lose money. But "the labs" and "compute demand" are not the same risk. What threatens the labs, no moats and a drift to open-weight models, hurts the labs' economics. It does not make the compute go away, because open models still run on GPUs and cheaper models pull in more use. The risk to NVIDIA runs through financing, whether the companies that sign the multi-year contracts can keep paying them, not through tokens disappearing. That is a narrower claim than his, and it is testable.

There is a circular version of this, that NVIDIA funds the labs that buy its compute. The 10-Q shows NVIDIA has committed to equity investments in "AI model makers, infrastructure financiers, and other private companies," on top of roughly $99 billion of equity investments on its books. Huang's answer at Goldman Sachs was that "the circular part of it doesn't make much sense to me because the number shows it doesn't." I would hold him to the number. It is a real exposure, sized in the ledger, not proof of a fraud.

One risk sits outside this post, and I want to be honest that I am leaving it out rather than pretend it is small. The labs could be forced to stop, not by economics but by a catastrophic AI event and whatever follows it. Both OpenAI and Anthropic are now openly planning for that scenario. I cannot put a number on it, so it is not in my ledger and it does not belong in a valuation. It is the subject of a separate post. Here I am pricing the money.

## What the multiple is already pricing

The strange part of this trade is that the stock is not priced for the bull case. NVIDIA trades at about 14.81x next-twelve-months EV/EBITDA, down from 32.44x a year ago, on a business that just grew 106% and guided to its first-ever full-year number. Microsoft, growing far slower, trades at 16.81x. The de-rating has already happened.

That matters because Burry's puts do not strictly need rent to fall. The market can simply decide to price the end of the shortage early, assigning NVIDIA a lower multiple now because it expects growth to slow later, and the stock falls on the multiple alone, with earnings intact. You can watch that mechanism live in memory. Samsung guided to a record quarterly profit near $80 billion on October 8, its fourth straight record, and the stock is down more than 25% from its June high. Micron trades at 6.1x forward earnings and Samsung at 4.2x, the way markets price a business they think is near its peak.

But here is the thing the 14.81x tells you, and it is the heart of my position. That is the multiple of a business the market expects to slow down, not one it expects to break. If the market genuinely thought a lab failure was coming, the kind that takes a quarter of next year's business with it, NVIDIA would trade where Micron does, at a single-digit multiple. It does not. So the lab-failure risk, the only one that actually reaches NVIDIA, is not in the price. That cuts both ways. If it happens, the stock falls hard, and Burry wins on the multiple rather than on rent. If it does not, the stock is cheap for what it earns. I am on the second side of that, because I do not think a lab fails to fund itself before September 2027, with memory short, orders booked, and the biggest buyers self-financing.

There is a cleaner way to see the two forces. One is the discount rate: the 10-year Treasury yield was 5.24% this week, and higher rates lower the value of distant earnings. But at about 16.5x forward earnings, NVIDIA is not a 2035 story, so rising rates hurt it less than a 40x software name. The other is the cycle, whether this decade's earnings hold after the shortage, and that is the who-pays question wearing a different hat. The multiple is pricing the first. It is barely pricing the second.

Three candidate rules I am weighing, and want reader input on before I set them:

- **Lab revenue.** OpenAI net annualized revenue below $70 billion at the end of 2026, the figure it has guided to during its raise. A clean "who pays" test. The objection: it is a private number I cannot audit.
- **Lab funding.** A frontier-lab funding round that is cut in size or pulled, or a neocloud refinancing that prices materially wider than the last. The objection: "materially" needs a number before it is a rule.
- **Lab share.** NVIDIA's AI-lab share of the business, which Kress put at roughly a quarter, falling on two consecutive calls. The objection: a fall could be good news if the hyperscalers are growing faster.

## The close

Burry rolled his puts to September 2027, toward the supply wave where the memory argument runs out. When supply does catch up, what decides NVIDIA is not what the old chips are worth. A gigawatt pays back its chips in two years and earns for six, rent is rising, and memory stays short, so the shortage ending leaves years of room before it reaches the company that sells the chips. What decides NVIDIA is whether the labs renting the compute can keep paying, and the market has not priced that failure, because if it had, the stock would be trading at a memory-maker's multiple. On October 8 it showed you the order it checks in: the labs first, the clouds next, NVIDIA last.

I am still holding NVIDIA. The thing that would change that is not a fall in GPU rent. It is a fall in what the AI labs can spend. So the one question I would put to you is the one I am watching myself: which of those two do you think moves first?
