# The Shortage Will End. It Won't Touch NVIDIA.

*The question everyone is asking about NVIDIA is what happens when the memory shortage ends. My answer is almost nothing. Rent on old chips falls, and NVIDIA sells the replacement at a higher price. The risk that actually decides the stock is whether the AI labs keep paying for the compute, and this week the market started pricing it in the credit of the companies around NVIDIA, not in the rent on a used chip.*

---

Here is the answer, before the argument. When the memory shortage ends, almost nothing happens to NVIDIA.

That is not the consensus. The consensus, and Michael Burry's trade, is that the shortage is the thing holding the AI complex up, so when it breaks, rent falls, NVIDIA's pricing cracks, and the stock goes with it. In late September, according to Stocktwits, Burry rolled his NVIDIA short into September 2027 puts, which moves his clock toward the 2028 supply wave, exactly where that bet pays. I think it reads the business backwards, and the past two weeks are the clearest evidence yet of why.

Since October 8 the AI trade has sold off hard, on a pile of things at once: a scare about OpenAI's revenue, a Chinese memory breakthrough that threatens the shortage, and the hottest inflation print in months. By October 16, CoreWeave was down about 30% from its October 7 high and Nebius about 29%, CoreWeave's cost of default insurance had hit a record, and some of its bonds yielded 16%. NVIDIA fell about 9%. Whatever set it off, the order it sold in is the tell. The companies that borrowed to buy the chips took the damage first. The company that sold them took a third of it.

[C2: NVDA, CoreWeave and Nebius indexed to October 7, the lessors falling three times as far as the chipmaker.]

That order is the whole thesis. NVIDIA is the largest weight in the S&P 500, so if you own an index fund you own this question, and almost everyone is answering the wrong version of it. The question is not what happens to rent when the shortage ends. It is whether the people renting the compute can keep paying, and only that reaches NVIDIA.

## The short version

- **What happened:** Since October 8 the AI trade sold off in order of who holds the financing risk. CoreWeave and Nebius fell about 30% and CoreWeave's default insurance hit a record, while NVIDIA fell about 9%. The trigger was a mix, but the order was the point.
- **Why the shortage ending barely touches NVIDIA:** On the deals the neoclouds are signing now, a gigawatt of compute brings in $20 to $25 million per megawatt a year and pays back its chips in about two years, against a six-year life. Rent would have to fall by half before a chip stops covering its cost, and rent is still rising. The building keeps its power, the cloud swaps in NVIDIA's next chip, and NVIDIA sells every upgrade at a higher price per gigawatt.
- **The number that matters:** Whether the labs keep paying. This week it split in two. Anthropic told investors it is now profitable and makes $1.70 of revenue for every $1 of compute. CoreWeave's credit market priced a better-than-even chance it defaults.
- **The verdict:** A hold, and a buy on weakness, on one condition. Not that the shortage lasts. That the labs keep paying.
- **What would prove me wrong:** A neocloud that cannot refinance, OpenAI's funding round shrinking, or ACIE revenue falling quarter over quarter. Not a markdown in what the old chips are worth.

## Why the shortage ending can't touch NVIDIA

Burry's bet needs rent to fall, and it will. The last time a memory shortage broke, the H100 index fell 44% in five months. The mistake is assuming that reaches the company that sells the chips, because the scarce asset is not the chip. It is the building with power running to it.

You can see which asset is scarce in the lead times. A gas turbine ordered today is quoted for around 2031, and large transformers run three to five years (a16z's September deck, citing TrendForce); power and permits take years more. The chip inside is the fast part, swapped every few years. Microsoft just showed you how it values the two: on its July call it extended the depreciation of its data centers from 15 years to 25, while it writes the servers inside them off over as little as two to six. NVIDIA's own 10-Q guarantees the "land, power, and shell" of its cloud partners' leases. The building is the long-lived asset; the silicon is the consumable.

So a neocloud is a bet on a powered site, and the chip is the part it refreshes. That turns Burry's 1968 computer-leasing analogy inside out twice. First, the old chip does not have to hold its rent, it has to be replaced, and every replacement is a sale for NVIDIA at a rising price: its revenue per gigawatt has gone from $18 billion with Hopper to $25 billion with Blackwell to $40 billion with Rubin, and the roadmap carries it to $60 billion with Feynman. Second, IBM wanted its leasing companies dead, but NVIDIA wants its neoclouds alive: it owns about 10% of CoreWeave, guarantees its partners' leases, and is building financing platforms to funnel capital to them. The chipmaker is propping up its own lessors. That is a reason NVIDIA is hit last, and it is also the circularity the bears point at, so I will come back to it.

Does the refresh actually pay? The neoclouds' own books say yes. Nebius said in August its multi-year deals yield $20 to $25 million per megawatt with a payback under two years; IREN said the same, with three-year pricing up about 125% from November 2025 to its August call, and Nebius raised list prices again on October 1. A gigawatt earning $20 to $25 billion a year pays back $40 billion of Rubin chips in about two years, against a six-year life. **For the upgrade to stop making sense, rent has to fall by half, and right now it is rising.** A 44% fall, the worst the H100 has ever shown, lands inside that cushion, not through it. The shortage ending just takes out a scarcity premium the business does not need.

## The thing that can touch it: who pays

NVIDIA keeps selling as long as the clouds keep buying, and the clouds keep buying as long as their customers keep paying. Follow that money and the risk sorts itself. NVIDIA's biggest customers are the hyperscalers, Microsoft, Amazon and Google, which buy GPUs out of their own cash flow and were about $49 billion of its $89 billion of data-center revenue last quarter. The riskier slice is the other $40 billion, what NVIDIA calls ACIE, which includes the neoclouds. The neoclouds borrow to buy the chips and rent them to the labs, so that is the part of NVIDIA's revenue that runs back to whether the labs can pay. NVIDIA's 10-Q says five direct customers are 70% of its receivables, up from three at 56% a year earlier, and it does not name them, but by scale they are the hyperscalers and the manufacturers that build for them, not the neoclouds. So the lab-financing risk, the one everyone is now worried about, threatens less than half of NVIDIA's data-center revenue, and the safer half is self-funded. That is the first reason I am not panicking.

Within the riskier half, the two labs that anchor it told opposite stories this week. Anthropic, days before its November IPO, said it is now profitable on an adjusted basis and makes $1.70 of revenue for every $1 of compute. Treat the profit as an IPO-roadshow number, adjusted and self-reported, and remember it has its own $518 billion compute commitment, but the direction is real: a lab that lost more than $8 billion last year is close to paying its own way. OpenAI is the one that still burns, planning $856 billion of compute through 2030 against $278 billion of negative cash flow, so it depends on raising money, and this week it is raising into a market with higher yields and less appetite. The near-term financing risk sits with OpenAI, not Anthropic.

Which is why the thing that actually cracked was credit, not rent. CoreWeave's five-year default insurance hit a record and its bonds hit 16%. Read the debt, though, and the scare is narrower than the headline. The 16% is on CoreWeave's unsecured notes, the ones with no contract or chips behind them, which sit behind all its other debt and are a minority of a roughly $35 billion stack. The money that actually funds the chips is secured against named customer contracts and rated investment grade: its $8.5 billion Meta-backed facility priced at 5.9%, anchored by Blackstone. Those secured loans are private, so I cannot watch them tick the way I can watch the bonds, but their investment-grade ratings are why the stress is showing up in the unsecured sliver and the insurance, not in a visible repricing of the core. This is the opposite of 2006, when the risk hid behind safe ratings. Here it is priced in daylight. It is stress, not default, though I would not lean on how comforting that is: Jensen Huang said on October 15 that CoreWeave's capital costs are falling and his company, which owns 10% of it, would not bail it out, the stock bounced 6% that day, and then it fell another 12% the next. The reassurance did not hold.

Stress is the thing to watch, and it is why the number is credit, not rent. Post 1 already found the credit market "charging for the risk" rather than hiding it; this is that, louder. A neocloud that cannot roll its debt stops buying chips long before any rent index moves, so the warning comes from CoreWeave's bonds, the size and price of OpenAI's next raise, and ACIE revenue, which prints in November. Not from the Silicon Data tape.

## The verdict

So: when the memory shortage ends, what happens to NVIDIA, and is it a hold or a buy?

When the shortage ends, almost nothing happens to the business. Rent on old chips falls, the neocloud swaps in NVIDIA's next chip, which pays for itself in two years and earns for six, and NVIDIA books the sale at a higher price per gigawatt. The scarcity premium comes out of the multiple, which is already happening: NVIDIA trades at about 18 times forward earnings, down from 33 a year ago, on earnings the street still sees growing more than 80% a year over the next two. That is the price of a business the market expects to slow, not one it expects to break. If it expected a lab to fail and take a quarter of next year's revenue, NVIDIA would trade where Micron does, at six times. It does not, even after this week, which means the one risk that reaches NVIDIA is still not in the price.

**So NVIDIA is a hold, and a buy on weakness, on one condition. Not that the shortage lasts. That the labs keep paying.** The shortage ending does not make it a sell; it makes it cheaper for the same earnings. A lab that stops paying is the only thing that makes it a sell, and that is the number I am watching, in CoreWeave's bonds and OpenAI's next raise, not in the rent on a used H100. This week the fundamentals under the stock improved, Anthropic turned a profit and rent kept rising, while the price got worse. I will take that trade, with my eyes on the credit tape, not adding with both hands.

One caveat I owe you, because it is the bear's best card. Gartner said this week that 80% of neoclouds will not survive to 2030 and that $3.5 trillion of AI infrastructure is exposed to write-down. The 80% is real, but read what "not survive independently" means: when a neocloud fails, its powered buildings and its signed contracts get absorbed by a stronger operator, and the compute inside still gets bought from NVIDIA. That is my read of consolidation, not Gartner's words, but it is the building thesis from the bankruptcy side. The risk is real for a neocloud's equity and debt, which is what this week was about. It is a smaller risk for the company that sells the chips into whoever owns the building next.

## The close

Burry rolled his puts toward the shortage ending, and he may get his crash in the shortage. He will not get it in NVIDIA, because the shortage was never NVIDIA's risk. The risk is a lab that runs out of money, and this week the market started pricing it in the one place it shows up first: not the rent on the oldest chip, but the cost of insuring the newest lender's debt. So that is the number to watch. Which do you think moves first, CoreWeave's credit or an H100's rent?
