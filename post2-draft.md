# The Shortage Won't Break NVIDIA. The Labs Might.

*The shortage ending barely touches NVIDIA. What decides the stock is whether the AI labs can keep paying for the compute, and this week the market started pricing that in credit, not in rent.*

---

Here is the answer, before the argument. When the memory shortage ends, almost nothing happens to NVIDIA.

That is not the consensus. The consensus, and Michael Burry's trade, is that the shortage is the thing holding the AI complex up, so when it breaks, rent falls, NVIDIA's pricing cracks, and the stock goes with it. In late September, according to Stocktwits, Burry rolled his NVIDIA short into September 2027 puts, which moves his clock toward the 2028 supply wave, exactly where that bet pays. I think it reads the business backwards, and the past two weeks are the clearest evidence yet of why.

Since October 8 the AI trade has sold off hard, on a pile of things at once: a scare about OpenAI's revenue, a Chinese memory breakthrough that threatens the shortage, and the hottest inflation print in months. By October 16, CoreWeave was down about 30% from its October 7 high and Nebius about 29%, CoreWeave's cost of default insurance had hit a record, and some of its bonds yielded 16%. NVIDIA fell about 9%. Whatever set it off, the order it sold in is the tell. The companies that borrowed to buy the chips took the damage first. The company that sold them took a third of it.

[C2: NVDA, CoreWeave and Nebius indexed to October 7, the lessors falling three times as far as the chipmaker.]

That order is the whole thesis. NVIDIA is the largest weight in the S&P 500, so if you own an index fund you own this question, and almost everyone is answering the wrong version of it. The question is not what happens to rent when the shortage ends. It is whether the people renting the compute can keep paying, and only that reaches NVIDIA.

## The short version

- **What happened:** Since October 8 the AI trade sold off in order of who holds the financing risk. CoreWeave and Nebius fell about 30% and CoreWeave's default insurance hit a record, while NVIDIA fell about 9%. The trigger was a mix, but the order was the point.
- **Why the shortage ending barely touches NVIDIA:** On the deals the neoclouds are signing now, a gigawatt of compute earns its chips back well inside their six-year life. On my conservative method, rent would have to fall by about half before a chip stops covering its cost, and rent is still rising. The building keeps its power, the cloud swaps in NVIDIA's next chip, and NVIDIA sells every upgrade at a higher price per gigawatt.
- **The number that matters:** Whether the labs keep paying. This week cut both ways. Anthropic, a lab, said on a self-reported adjusted basis that it is now profitable and makes $1.70 of revenue for every $1 of compute. Meanwhile the credit market priced a better-than-even chance that CoreWeave, the most levered lessor, defaults.
- **The verdict:** A hold, and a buy on weakness, on one condition. Not that the shortage lasts. That the labs keep paying.
- **What would prove me wrong:** A neocloud that cannot refinance, OpenAI's funding round shrinking, or ACIE revenue falling quarter over quarter. Not a markdown in what the old chips are worth.

## Why the shortage ending can't touch NVIDIA

Burry's bet needs rent to fall, and it will. The last time a GPU shortage eased and new chips flooded in, the H100 index fell 44% in five months. It could even end sooner than the memory makers claim: a Chinese maker, CXMT, says it has a denser DRAM design shipping this year, and Acer's chief says the shortage has "completely disappeared." I take those seriously, and they do not change my answer, because the mistake is assuming the shortage ending reaches the company that sells the chips. It does not, because the scarce asset is not the chip. It is the building with power running to it.

You can see which asset is scarce in the lead times. A gas turbine ordered today is quoted for around 2031, and large transformers run three to five years (a16z's September deck, slide 55); power and permits take years more. The chip inside is the fast part, swapped every few years. Microsoft just showed you how it values the two: on its July call it extended the depreciation of its data centers from 15 years to 25, while it writes the servers inside them off over as little as two to six. NVIDIA's own 10-Q guarantees the "land, power, and shell" of its cloud partners' leases. The building is the long-lived asset; the silicon is the consumable.

The neoclouds mostly rent those buildings rather than own them, which is why CoreWeave carries $16 billion of leases, and why a developer like SB Energy can plan a $50 billion listing on the shells and the power alone. The durable asset has its own owners now, separate from the chips inside it and the operators who fill them.

So a neocloud is a bet on a powered site, and the chip is the part it refreshes. That turns Burry's 1968 computer-leasing analogy inside out twice. First, the old chip does not have to hold its rent, it has to be replaced, and every replacement is a sale for NVIDIA at a rising price: its revenue per gigawatt has gone from $18 billion with Hopper to $25 billion with Blackwell to $40 billion with Rubin, and the roadmap carries it to $60 billion with Feynman. Second, IBM wanted its leasing companies dead, but NVIDIA wants its neoclouds alive: it owns about 10% of CoreWeave, guarantees its partners' leases, and is building financing platforms to funnel capital to them. The chipmaker is propping up its own lessors. That is a reason NVIDIA is hit last, and it is also the circularity the bears point at, so I will come back to it.

Does the refresh pay? The operators say it does. Nebius and IREN both report their multi-year deals, at $20 to $25 million per megawatt, earning the cost of the chips back in under two years, and three-year pricing is rising, not falling: IREN's is up about 125% since November 2025, and Nebius raised list prices again on October 1. I will not lean on their two-year figure, because they do not say whether it is before or after the operating costs that take roughly 40% of rent. On the stricter net basis I used for the B200 last time, at 70% utilization and after those costs, a chip earns its cost back inside its six-year life as long as rent does not fall by more than about half. **That is the cushion, and rent is rising, not falling.** A 44% drop, the worst the H100 has ever shown, lands inside it. The shortage ending just removes a scarcity premium the business does not need.

## The thing that can touch it: who pays

NVIDIA keeps selling as long as the clouds keep buying, and the clouds keep buying as long as their customers keep paying. Follow that money and the risk sorts itself. NVIDIA's biggest customers are the hyperscalers, Microsoft, Amazon and Google, which back their GPU buying with the strongest balance sheets in the world, even as their free cash flow thins and they lean more on debt and leases to do it, and which were about $49 billion of its $89 billion of data-center revenue last quarter. The riskier slice is the other $40 billion, what NVIDIA calls ACIE, which includes the neoclouds. The neoclouds borrow to buy the chips and rent them out, to the labs and to the hyperscalers' overflow, so that is the part of NVIDIA's revenue that runs back to whether those renters can pay. NVIDIA's 10-Q says five direct customers are 70% of its receivables, up from three at 56% a year earlier. It does not name them; by its own customer categories they are the clouds and the manufacturers that assemble the racks, firms like Super Micro and Dell, not the neoclouds. I am inferring that, and the filing confirms only the concentration. So the lab-financing risk, the one everyone is now worried about, threatens less than half of NVIDIA's data-center revenue, and the safer half sits with investment-grade giants that can fund it in almost any market, not with the levered neoclouds. That is the first reason I am not panicking.

Within the riskier half, the two labs that anchor it told opposite stories this week. Anthropic, days before an IPO it is targeting for November, said it is now profitable on an adjusted basis it did not define and that it makes $1.70 of revenue for every $1 it spends on compute, a gross figure that counts the compute bill and not its people or its research. Treat the profit as an IPO-roadshow number, self-reported, and remember Anthropic has its own $518 billion compute commitment, but the direction is real: a lab that lost more than $8 billion last year is close to paying its own way. OpenAI is the one that still burns, planning $856 billion of compute through 2030 against $278 billion of negative cash flow, so it depends on raising money, and this week it is raising into a market with higher yields and less appetite. The near-term financing risk sits with OpenAI, not Anthropic.

You could watch that risk move this week in a place that touches NVIDIA directly. SB Energy, the SoftBank developer building OpenAI's Ohio campus, the one NVIDIA both guarantees and has put $3 billion into, stalled its roughly $50 billion listing when investors balked at the valuation and at how heavily it leans on OpenAI. That is the who-pays question showing up in a financing attached to NVIDIA's single largest guarantee, and it is a genuine mark against me, not a footnote: the funding market is starting to price the concentration I say is the real risk.

Which is why the stress showed up in credit, not rent, and the difference is the whole point. Rent is the price of using a GPU, and it is still rising, because demand is strong. Credit is the price these companies pay to borrow, and that is what cracked, because the worry is not what a chip rents for, it is whether the levered companies that bought it can keep funding themselves. CoreWeave's five-year default insurance hit a record and its bonds hit 16%. Its June 10-Q tells you what that 16% is and is not. CoreWeave carries about $35 billion of debt, plus another $16 billion of data-center leases, which are the rent it owes on buildings it does not own, not borrowed money. By my weighting of the per-facility rates the filing discloses, the debt sits at a blended effective rate near 8%. The 16% is the market yield on its unsecured senior notes, the ones with no collateral behind them, about $10 billion of face value issued around 10% and now trading down, so CoreWeave still pays the old coupon and the 16% is only what a new buyer collects. The rest of the stack is cheaper and safer: about $12 billion of term loans secured against named customer contracts, including the investment-grade, Meta-backed facility priced around 6% and anchored by Blackstone, plus about $7 billion of convertible notes at 2%; and the $2.6 billion DDTL 5.5 my last post covered comes on top of those June figures. So the viral number is the most expensive, most subordinated slice quoted as if it were the whole company, whose real cost of debt is nearer 8%. And it is a rolling obligation, not a single lump due at once: the secured loans amortize monthly, about $6 billion over the next year, while the unsecured notes do not mature until 2030 and beyond. The stress is the price of new money and of that steady paydown, not a wall CoreWeave has to refinance all at once. That is the opposite of 2006, when the risk hid behind safe ratings. Here it is priced in daylight, and it is stress, not default, though I would not lean on how comforting that is. Jensen Huang said on October 15 that CoreWeave's capital costs are falling and his company, which owns 10% of it, would not bail it out; the stock bounced 6% that day and fell another 12% the next. The reassurance did not hold.

Stress is the thing to watch, and it is why the number is credit, not rent. Post 1 already found the credit market "charging for the risk" rather than hiding it; this is that, louder. A neocloud that cannot roll its debt stops buying chips long before any rent index moves, so the warning comes from CoreWeave's bonds, the size and price of OpenAI's next raise, and ACIE revenue, which prints in November. Not from the Silicon Data tape.

## The verdict

So: when the memory shortage ends, what happens to NVIDIA, and is it a hold or a buy?

When the shortage ends, almost nothing happens to the business. Rent on old chips falls, the neocloud swaps in NVIDIA's next chip, which earns its cost back well inside its six-year life even after operating costs, and NVIDIA books the sale at a higher price per gigawatt. The scarcity premium comes out of the multiple, and it is happening in real time: NVIDIA trades at about 14 times forward earnings, down from the 16.5 I quoted two weeks ago and from above 30 a year ago. That is the price of a business the market expects to slow, not one it expects to break. If it expected a lab to fail and take a quarter of next year's revenue, NVIDIA would trade where Micron does, at six times forward earnings. It does not, even after this week, which means the one risk that reaches NVIDIA is still not in the price.

**So NVIDIA is a hold, and a buy on weakness, on one condition. Not that the shortage lasts. That the labs keep paying.** The shortage ending does not make it a sell; it makes it cheaper for the same earnings. A lab that stops paying is the only thing that makes it a sell, and that is the number I am watching, in CoreWeave's bonds and OpenAI's next raise, not in the rent on a used H100. I will be precise about this week, because it cut both ways. One lab moved toward profitability and rent kept rising, which is the demand case. Against that, the most levered lessor's credit stress hit a record, a Chinese memory threat emerged, and SB Energy's listing stalled on OpenAI concentration. I weight the first two for NVIDIA because they speak to demand and the chip economics, and the credit stress, so far, is stress and not default. The demand case held while the price fell, so I will take that trade, with my eyes on the credit tape, not adding with both hands.

One caveat I owe you, because it is the bear's best card. Gartner said this week that 80% of neoclouds will "cease independent operations" by 2030 and that $3.5 trillion of AI infrastructure is exposed to write-down. The number is real; read the phrase. It was never about which neocloud survives, because the scarce thing is the powered building, and the building stays. When a neocloud fails, a stronger operator or a hyperscaler buys its buildings and its contracts, so 80% consolidating into 20% means the survivors own more powered sites, and buy more NVIDIA chips, than the failures did apart. The transition is not free: a neocloud in a forced sale is not buying new Rubin while it is carved up, its assets change hands at a markdown, and its contracts can sit in dispute, so a wave of failures could stall NVIDIA's order book for a stretch even if it does not shrink the end demand. The write-down lands on a neocloud's equity and debt, which is what this week was about. It is a far smaller, and more temporary, risk for the company that sells the chips into whoever owns the building next. That last step is my read of what consolidation means, not Gartner's.

## Break conditions

Rules 1 to 5 carry forward from my August 28 post, word for word. Rules 6 to 9 are from October 5. Rule 10 is new. I grade each as written. Rule 6 I now read alongside rule 8, since a falling B200 index can just mean the next chip arrived rather than demand weakening.

1. Non-GAAP gross margin below 72% for two consecutive quarters during the Vera Rubin ramp. Set Jun 2026. Action: exit review. Not triggered. Q2 75.0%; Q4 guided 71 to 72%. One quarter below 72% is guided, so Q1 FY28 is the test.

2. ACIE/Hyperscale split reversing: Hyperscale reaccelerates while ACIE decelerates. Set Jun 2026. Action: exit review. Not triggered. ACIE +25% q/q vs Hyperscale +13%.

3. Any hyperscaler framing custom silicon as primary AI training compute on two consecutive calls, combined with a sequential decline in Hyperscale revenue. Set Jun 2026. Action: exit review. Not triggered.

4. Vera CPU shipments delayed beyond Q1 FY2028. Set Jun 2026. Action: exit review. Not triggered. Shipping to lead partners.

5. FY2028 revenue guide walked back below 60% growth on any subsequent call. Set Aug 28, 2026. Action: exit review. Not triggered. Guided 70%.

6. GPU rent: the Silicon Data B200 index for three consecutive monthly readings before September 2027, below $3.93 for a review and below $2.85 for an exit. Set Oct 5, 2026. Not triggered. About $5.86 on October 2. Read alongside rule 8.

7. Memory supply: Micron, Samsung or SK hynix says memory supply and demand balance in calendar 2027. Set Oct 5, 2026. Action: exit review. Not triggered. All three still say tighter in 2027, though CXMT's denser DRAM and Acer's "shortage gone" are the first signals pointing the other way.

8. Lessor stress reaches the order book: ACIE revenue declines quarter over quarter. Set Oct 5, 2026. Action: exit review. Not triggered. +25% q/q in Q2; the Q3 print lands in November.

9. Guarantees: NVIDIA records any payment or loss under its AI cloud, land/power/shell, SB Energy or residual-value support arrangements. Set Oct 5, 2026. Action: review and resize. Not triggered, though SB Energy's stalled IPO sits behind the largest of them.

10. Neocloud financing: a CoreWeave or peer neocloud refinancing that fails to clear, or a neocloud publicly guiding that its new funding costs more than its contract spread. Set Oct 18, 2026. Action: review. Not triggered. CoreWeave's secured debt is still clearing; the stress is in the unsecured bonds. I set no yield threshold, because a neocloud that signs at a high rate has presumably modelled that the rental still covers it, so the signal is whether the deals clear at all.

## The close

Burry rolled his puts toward the shortage ending, and he may get his crash in the shortage. He will not get it in NVIDIA, because the shortage was never NVIDIA's risk. The risk is a lab that runs out of money, and this week the market started pricing it in the one place it shows up first: not the rent on the oldest chip, but the cost of insuring the newest lender's debt. So watch the credit tape. If CoreWeave's next raise prices sharply wider or fails to clear, or OpenAI's round shrinks, the thesis is under stress, and I will say so here. If they clear, NVIDIA is a 14-times business the market has already de-rated and still refuses to break, and I will keep buying the weakness. That is the trade.

If you are watching one number to tell me I am wrong, I would watch CoreWeave's next raise. What is yours?
