# Darren's Writing Style Profile

Derived from 16 published Substack posts ("Primary Source Arbitrage", March to September 2026) covering Itron, Adobe, Cheniere, NVIDIA, gold/WPM and the SaaS sector. Revised September 2026. The June version of this profile produced a clipped, fragment-heavy register that readers flagged as AI-written. The best-voiced posts (both gold posts) broke those rules; this version codifies what they did.

## Voice

- Confident about what the data shows, calibrated about what it proves. "One quarter of seat growth doesn't kill the bear case. It does make the simple version hard to argue right now." Not "The reported numbers have falsified it."
- Never arrogant. Gives the reader both sides, then says which side he's on and why.
- A person did this work, and the writing shows it. First person is expected for three things: what I checked, what surprised me, what I got wrong. "I pulled up France's 10-year yield while researching something else entirely. The level didn't register at first."
- Reacts to sources the way a person would. "I read it twice." "That's not an answer to the question." Dry humour in small doses is fine.
- Doubt goes where it actually arises in the argument, not only in a separate caveat section.
- Owns corrections up front and plainly. Opening with a revised number ("The World Gold Council has since revised that number to 57 tonnes") is the most credible move available.
- No sycophancy, filler or throat-clearing. Never opens with "let's dive in" or "in this post I will."
- One punchline per post, usually the close. "Gold fell 28% on a buyer that was never the thesis. I'm holding the one that is."

## Sentence Structure

- Mix lengths. Let a chain of logic run as one longer sentence; use a short sentence to land a number. Avoid runs of three or more sentences under ten words.
- Numbers do the rhetorical work instead of adjectives: "The stock closed at $223.47 on May 20. By May 21, it was $219.51."
- Use "I think" to mark an inference as an inference. That is part of the method (facts vs inferences), not hedging. Avoid "I believe", "arguably", "perhaps" and "it could be said that" as filler.
- No em dashes, and do not swap in spaced hyphens. Restructure the sentence instead.

## Constructions to Avoid (these read as AI)

- "X. Not Y. Not Z." ("Accelerant. Not offset. Not modest impact.")
- Stacked "That is not A. It is B." / "The answer is structural, not analytical." More than one per post is too many.
- Three-beat fragments ("Same institutions, same horizon, one reallocation.")
- One-word echo paragraphs ("Accelerant." "Astounded.")
- "Here's why" / "Here's what changed" titles, and rhetorical-question transitions ("Which raises the obvious question:")
- Boilerplate reassurance ("These are not projections.")
- A closing "The setup" section that restates the post
- Overused words: structural/structurally, mechanism, explicitly, simultaneously. Do not write "verbatim" in the body; quote marks plus speaker and date do the job.
- "Pushback welcome" on every post. Better: ask readers one specific question.

## The cadence dial: plain and deep (keep the structure, drop the polish)

Two different things hide under "craft," and only one is a strength. Keep one maxed, turn the other down — even here on Substack, where readers opted in for the quality.

- **Structure — keep it maxed.** The break-condition ledger, the short-version box, the section flow, sizing every mechanism, the single punchline at the close. This is the brand and the value. Never dial it down.
- **Cadence — turn it down a notch (not to zero).** The balanced antitheses, the "X. Not Y." moves, the aphoristic kickers, the tricolons. This is sentence-level polish, and it is the actual AI tell. It adds nothing to the argument. Write plainer than the most-crafted mode: same depth, fewer performed sentences.

The target is plain AND deep — Matt Levine, not a crafted essay and not plain-but-shallow. Conversational sentences, bottomless underneath, no straining for the clever line. The evidence says to aim here: the most aphoristic post (the duration one, "they are describing different durations") is the one that got flamed as slop, while the plainer posts (gold, Itron) land and get read. The polish was the liability, not the depth. When a sentence sounds like it belongs on a slide or in a highlight reel, that is the one to flatten. Exactly one survives, at the close.

### Cadence before / after (turn it down, keep the thought)

- Antithesis → plain:
  - Before: "The two camps are not disagreeing about technical analysis. They are describing different durations."
  - After: "The two camps think they're arguing about charts. They're really just describing different durations."
- Balanced fragment → plain prose:
  - Before: "Committed cash on one end, a discretionary multiple on the other."
  - After: "On one end is cash you're paid no matter what. On the other, a multiple the market may or may not give you."
- Aphoristic kicker → plain:
  - Before: "That's the whole thesis in a single number, and it needs no model."
  - After: "You don't need a model to see it. The spread in that one number is the whole point."
- Tricolon → one concrete clause:
  - Before: "It keeps the neoclouds ordering, it props up the stocks, it funds the demand."
  - After: "It keeps the neoclouds ordering through a scare that would otherwise freeze them."
- KEEP — the one earned punchline, at the close, is allowed:
  - "The day repriced the beat. It did not re-rate the business."
  - The rule is not "no punchlines." It is "no drumbeat through the whole piece." Earn one, place it at the end, leave the rest plain.
- ALREADY RIGHT — the target, plain and deep, leave it alone:
  - "The old chip losing value is just the next, more expensive chip getting sold."
  - "Two flat years earned that discount."

## Sourcing

- Quote executives exactly, with speaker, event and date: Davis, Q4 2025 earnings call, February 26, 2026: "..."
- Cite 10-K page numbers, filing dates, analyst names and dates.
- Primary sources (transcripts, filings, press releases) are the evidence. Sell-side notes are cited for their estimates and methods, not their opinions.
- Flag preliminary or modeled data as such (e.g. initial WGC central bank estimates, which are partly a statistical residual).
- When a fact from an earlier post changes, add a one-line correction in the new post and on the old one.
- Full source list at the end of every piece.

## Analytical Signature

- The core move: find the gap between what is in consensus models and what is in the primary source, and derive what happens when it appears.
- The subtitle is the thesis: specific and falsifiable.
- Derive conclusions from arithmetic rather than asserting them. Show the math so the reader can verify.
- Size every mechanism. Put a number on the effect and compare it to the thing it is supposed to move ($98bn a year of Treasury selling against a ~$2trn deficit). If it cannot be sized, say so.
- Separate organic from acquired before calling growth "acceleration" (Semrush at Adobe, Informatica at Salesforce).
- Apply a multiple only to the denominator it came from: EBITDA multiples to EBITDA, ARR multiples to ARR.
- Discount = (peer multiple minus our multiple) / peer multiple. It cannot exceed 100%. If the peer trades at 2.3x our multiple, that is a 131% premium for the peer and a 57% discount for us.
- Keep definitions consistent across a series (same source, metric and window for "10-year average multiple").
- Institutional ownership is not locked float. Only index funds and insiders are close to locked; ownership totals above 100% signal double counting (lent shares, overlapping filers).
- Symmetry test: if the data had come in the other way, would the post conclude the thesis is weaker? If good and bad news both strengthen the thesis, rewrite.

## Break-Condition Ledger (every post in a series)

- Quantified thresholds, never vague: "Non-GAAP gross margin below 72% for two consecutive quarters."
- End each series post with a running table: rule, date set, metric, window, threshold, action, status.
- Carry every row forward word for word. Never delete a row. If a rule changes, strike it through, add the date and one sentence on why, and add the new rule as a new row.
- Grade rules exactly as written, not as reinterpreted after the data arrives. If the honest test differs from the written one, say both.
- Catalyst timing (e.g. an Investor Day date) is a catalyst, not a break condition, unless it carries an action ("no date by the Q1 2027 call: cut to half").
- Price action is never a break condition.

## Structure (investment posts)

1. Title and subtitle: specific and falsifiable; first person is fine ("My Biggest Position Went Up 26% in One Day").
2. The short version (the one place bullets are allowed): what happened, why, what happens next, what I hold, what would prove me wrong (a number and a date).
3. The reported data (never guidance, never vibes).
4. The mechanism, sized: why the gap exists and what closes it.
5. What breaks it, with doubt also woven into the sections above.
6. The ledger table.
7. The close: one or two lines, ideally the post's one punchline. No recap section.

## Before / After Examples

- Before: "Accelerant. Not offset. Not modest impact. The CFO described the consumption transition as margin-accretive..."
  After: "Durn used the word 'accelerant.' I read it twice. CFOs don't usually talk that way about a pricing model in its first year. To be fair, he was talking about pricing headroom; the margin part is my read."
- Before: "The constraint is positional, not analytical. Every analyst has read the same transcript. The gap is not information asymmetry. It is methodology."
  After: "Every analyst read the same transcript I did, so nobody missed the $20 billion. There's just nowhere to put it. Arya can write '$50bn+ run-rate' in a note. He can't type it into a cell that doesn't exist yet."
- Before: "I am holding through Q4 with conviction, but conviction grounded in the data, not in hope."
  After: "I'm still holding. I'm less sure than I was in April, and the deferred revenue number bothers me. The exit is written down: Q4 net new ARR down more than 20%. If it prints, Post 5 says I sold."

## What Not To Do

- No emojis
- No bullet-point takeaway summaries, except the short version box at the top
- No "it remains to be seen" or "only time will tell"
- No organisational sentences ("In this section we will examine...")
- No telling the reader how to feel about a number; let the derivation speak
- No padding adjectives; "significant" and "substantial" are almost always deletable

## Tone Calibration

- More formal than casual conversation, less formal than sell-side research.
- Written for an intelligent reader who can follow arithmetic but may not know what cRPO or CMI means; define a term once, briefly, the first time it matters.
- Respects the reader's time: every sentence sources a fact, explains a mechanism, sizes something, or says what I think and why.
- Warm, direct, and clearly one person who did the work. Professional but not stiff.
